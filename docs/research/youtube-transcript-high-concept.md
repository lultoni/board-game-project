## Key takeaways

The video frames "high concept" as a survival skill: a way to stop yourself from drifting into a game that's *about everything and therefore about nothing*. Four guideposts:

1. **Focus — mechanical or narrative?** Pick the inception point. Both can co-exist in the final game, but you have to know which one the rest of the design springs *from*. Without that, you end up describing "six alien classes and a rhythm cooking minigame meets time travel" and nobody (including you) knows what the game is.
2. **Specificity — one big risk.** What's the *one* thing that sets this game apart? Doing one thing well > a million disparate mediocre things. Even games that look like a "pile of stuff" (Yakuza, CoD) actually orient that stuff around one central theme. Identify the one risk; let everything else serve it.
3. **Brevity — sentence, then paragraph.** If you can't summarise the game in one sentence, you're probably lost. If you can't summarise it in a paragraph, you definitely are. The summary should provoke follow-up questions, not answer them.
4. **Clarity — test the pitch itself, not just the game.** Tell people the high concept. Do they get it? Do they get *excited*? Do their follow-up questions show they understand what you're going for? If not, back to the drawing board — before you build anything.

**Meta-point:** the high concept is the thing you check every new feature against. It's not just a marketing line — it's the filter that lets you say no to the next 10 sub-systems you'll be tempted to add.

## Relevance to project — what is *my* high concept?

### Step 1: Mechanical or narrative? → **Mechanical, decisively.**

This game has no narrative pretensions. There's no story, no characters with arcs, no world to explore. Even the name is a placeholder. The game is `(GAME NAME)` — that's the tell: the inception point is mechanics, and identity (theme, art, names) will be backfilled later in Phase B (`game-identity-visual-naming.md`).

The Core Fantasy in `design-principles.md` is already framed mechanically: *"Discovering and executing clever spell/skill combos."* That's not a story. That's a *type of decision* you want the player to feel.

### Step 2: Specificity — what's the one big risk?

The candidate one-liners I can extract from the existing docs:

- **"Chess but with spell combos."** Easy to parse, but inaccurate — it understates the depth of the skill system and oversells the chess inheritance (no piece roles, no checkmate logic).
- **"A 2-player abstract-tactical board game where every move serves a setup that pays off in a clever combo."** Closer to Core Fantasy, but a mouthful and still describes the chassis.
- **"Perfect-information tactical game where the depth comes from skill combos, not from piece variety or hidden information."** Accurate but still defensive — defined by what it *isn't*.

The actual one big risk, named honestly: **the entire game is the spell/combo system. Everything else — the 10x10 grid, Guards, Champions, the King, the Bodyguard rule, Rune economy — is *chassis* to make the combo system shine.** That's the unique thing. Most "wizard chess" games make spells an *addition* to a chess-like skeleton. This game makes the skeleton subordinate to the spells.

That matches the memory note: *"the game IS the spells/combos, everything else is chassis. Does this make spell combos better?"*

### Step 3: Brevity — drafting the sentence

Here are some attempts, ordered from chassis-first to combo-first:

- (chassis-first) *"A 2-player abstract-tactical board game on a 10x10 grid where you draft skills and capture the enemy King."* — describes the box, not the game.
- (mechanic-first) *"A perfect-information board game whose depth comes from drafting and chaining skills across a small board."* — better, but "depth" is a claim, not a hook.
- (fantasy-first) **"A 2-player perfect-information board game where the only thing that matters is finding clever skill combos before your opponent does."** — this is the one that passes the Core Fantasy test. It tells you *what the player is doing*, not just what's on the table.

Paragraph-length expansion (for when someone asks the follow-up):
> Two players draft skills onto their pieces before the game starts. The board is a chess-sized grid; pieces move simply. The depth lives entirely in the skill system: skills cost a scarce currency, travel in straight lines that other pieces block, and combo with one another in ways the draft and the board position make possible. There's no luck, no hidden information, no dice. The whole game is a shared puzzle: who can find the better combo from the same open position?

### Step 4: Clarity — what would the test look like?

The pitch test from the video maps directly onto the playtest results we already have: when new players (Nico, the P1/P2/P3 testers) describe the game *back* after playing it, do they describe it in skill/combo terms, or in chess/movement terms? If they say *"it's like chess with spells,"* the high concept hasn't landed — they're seeing the chassis, not the engine. If they say *"it's about finding the right spell sequence,"* it has landed.

This is worth checking against existing playtest analyses before drafting the next stack.

## What this means for the design discipline going forward

The video reframes a lot of the existing hygiene principles in `CLAUDE.md`:

- **The Justification Rule** is the high-concept filter applied per-feature. *"Does this make spell combos better?"* is just *"does this serve the high concept?"*
- **"Cut features, deepen systems"** in design-principles is the specificity guidepost in disguise.
- **The 5 principles from ADR-003** are all downstream of the high concept being *combo discovery* — not *tactical positioning*, not *resource management*. Those are axes that *support* the combo experience, not co-equal pillars.

Concrete forward-looking applications:

1. **Every backpocket entry** should answer not just *"what does it fix?"* but *"how does it sharpen the combo experience?"* If a fix would solve a chassis problem at the cost of making combos less central, that's a red flag, not a feature.
2. **When a new sub-system is proposed** (e.g., terrain, piece variety, resource alternatives), apply the brevity test: *can the new one-sentence pitch still fit in a sentence?* If adding the system forces the pitch to grow a clause, the system is fighting the high concept.
3. **Stack design** should already privilege changes that affect the combo system (skill economy, draft, slot scaling, combo bonus) over changes to the chassis (board size, piece counts, movement rules). The current testing plan mostly does this — but worth re-checking that prioritisation explicitly against the high concept.
4. **The naming/identity phase** (Phase B) can now be briefed properly: the theme/art/name has to *serve a game whose core is clever combo discovery*. That immediately rules out theming that pulls toward war-chess (Battle Chess), narrative campaigns (Gloomhaven), or character-class identity (Summoner Wars). It pulls toward something more like *puzzle-arcane*, where the visual language reinforces "find the elegant solution."

### Open questions this raises

- Is *"clever combo discovery"* really the high concept, or is it *"clever combo discovery against an opponent who is solving the same puzzle"*? The second framing makes the 2-player nature load-bearing, not incidental. (See ADR / `cooperative-feel-competitive-games.md` research.)
- Does the current skill catalogue actually support enough combo discovery, or is it too narrow? — this is more of a content question, but the high concept makes it urgent.
- Is the 10x10 grid + 12 pieces the *minimum chassis* needed to host the combo system, or is it bigger than it needs to be? Specificity suggests checking whether *less* chassis would make combos *more* central.

## Work angles — what to do next

Four candidate threads to pull on, in order of recommended sequencing. Each one is a real piece of design work, not just a writing exercise.

### Angle 1 — Validate the high concept against existing playtest data *(starting here)*

**Question:** When testers describe the game *back* after playing, do they reach for combo-first language (*"it's about finding the right spell sequence"*) or chassis-first language (*"it's like chess with spells"*)?

**Why first:** Cheapest, most diagnostic. Free signal from data we already have. The answer reframes everything else: if combo-first, the high concept is real and the rest of the work is refinement; if chassis-first, the high concept hasn't landed *for actual humans* and the rest of the work changes character.

**Inputs:** `docs/research/playtest-1-analysis.md`, `playtest-2-analysis.md`, `playtest-3-analysis.md`, anything from Nico's session.

**Output:** A short verdict — landed / partially landed / not landed — with quotes/observations as evidence. Saved into this file as a new section.

### Angle 2 — Audit existing systems against the high concept

**Question:** Walk every system in `systems-and-mechanics.md` and ask *"does this serve combo discovery, or is it just chassis?"* For chassis systems: are they minimum-viable chassis or are they louder than they need to be?

**Why second:** Once Angle 1 tells us whether the high concept is landing, this audit becomes either a refinement pass (combos heard, tighten the chassis) or a problem-diagnosis pass (combos not heard, find what's drowning them out).

**Inputs:** `docs/systems-and-mechanics.md`, baseline ruleset.

**Output:** A per-system scorecard. Each system tagged combo-serving / chassis-necessary / chassis-bloat, with rationale. Bloat candidates feed into stack proposals.

### Angle 3 — Pressure-test the one-sentence pitch (ADR-shaped)

**Question:** Is the high concept *"clever combo discovery"* or *"clever combo discovery against an opponent solving the same puzzle"*? The second makes 2-player load-bearing — competition isn't a feature, it's part of the core. The first makes the game closer to a single-player puzzle game with an opponent acting as a constraint generator.

**Why this matters:** The two framings yield different design choices. Framing A leans into asymmetric information about *how each player is solving the puzzle* (no hidden info, but their skill draft is visible) and rewards reading the opponent's plan. Framing B leans into shared-board elegance, where the second player's role is to be the wall the puzzle is solved against.

**When to do it:** After Angle 1. If the playtest data already hints at one framing over the other, Angle 3 might collapse into a quick decision rather than a full ADR.

**Output:** Either an ADR with the framings as alternatives, or a one-paragraph decision in `design-principles.md` if the answer is obvious post-Angle-1.

### Angle 4 — Chassis minimisation

**Question:** Is the 10x10 grid + 12 pieces (1 King, 5 Champions, 6 Guards) the *minimum chassis* that hosts the combo system, or is it bigger than it needs to be? Could the same combo experience work on 8x8, with fewer Champions, without Guards?

**Why this matters:** Specificity says "do one thing well, cut what doesn't serve." If a smaller chassis hosts the same combo system, *less chassis = combos louder*. Conversely, if the chassis is already minimum-viable, this confirms it and we stop second-guessing.

**Why last:** Biggest, most speculative bet. Touches every other system. Worth doing — but only after Angles 1–3 have told us what the chassis is *for*. Premature minimisation is just deletion.

**Output:** An ADR comparing chassis sizes, with explicit criteria for what the chassis must support (draft size, combo radius, draw-condition timing, etc.).

## Angle 3 — Pressure-test the pitch: combo discovery alone vs. against an opponent solving the same puzzle

**Format:** Discussion first, ADR later if needed. The "decide now vs. wait for more data" question is itself deferred — we'll come back to it once the discussion has clarified what's actually at stake.

**Background reading:** `cooperative-feel-competitive-games.md` (the "shared-puzzle feel" research thread — Onitama, Twilight Struggle, Tigris & Euphrates, Tak, GIPF, Go).

### The two framings, stated cleanly

**Framing A — Solo-puzzle-with-opponent:**
*"A 2-player perfect-information board game where the only thing that matters is finding clever skill combos before your opponent does."*

The opponent is a **constraint generator**. Their pieces are obstacles, their threats are deadlines, their captures are setbacks. The player's experience is *me, solving the puzzle of this board*. The 2-player nature is a delivery mechanism for time pressure and consequence — replace the opponent with a sufficiently good AI or a timer-plus-puzzle-deck and the game survives.

**Framing B — Two minds, one puzzle:**
*"A 2-player perfect-information board game where two players race to discover and execute clever skill combos in a shared combinatorial space."*

The opponent is a **fellow puzzle-solver**. The combinatorial space is the same for both — same skill pool, same board, same rules — and the experience is *us, simultaneously navigating this puzzle, racing to see who finds the elegant move first*. The 2-player nature is **load-bearing**: replace the opponent with an AI and you lose the core experience, because the experience IS the parallel-solving.

This is the distinction the research file calls **mutual epistemic exploration**: both players knowing the other is solving the same shape, recognising elegant play in each other, sharing the "ah, of course" moment.

### Why this isn't just rhetoric

Same one-sentence pitch on the box; very different design implications downstream. Concretely:

| Design lever | Framing A implication | Framing B implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Skill draft** | Asymmetric drafts okay — each player gets their own puzzle to solve | Symmetric or near-symmetric drafts preferred — both players solving the *same* combinatorial space |
| **Combo legibility** | Your own combos must be legible to *you*; opponent legibility is secondary | Both players' combos must be legible to *both* — the opponent's clever move is supposed to land aesthetically |
| **Information transparency** | Already perfect-info, fine | Perfect-info + *visual* legibility (skill loadouts on table, Rune costs announced, combos performed openly) becomes load-bearing, not nice-to-have |
| **Designed analysis moments** | Optional; the puzzle is solved internally | Important — simultaneous-reveal phases, post-combat acknowledgement, kifu-style review all become more valuable |
| **Asymmetric factions** | Compatible — different puzzles per side | Tension — asymmetry weakens shared-puzzle feel unless both factions stay legible to each other |
| **Theme / Phase B** | "Arcane puzzler" — solo wizard solving an arcane puzzle | "Two minds, one puzzle" — duelling diviners, parallel oracle interpretation, mirrored mages reading the same constellation |
| **Opponent's role aesthetically** | Antagonist, obstacle, the wall | Mirror, witness, fellow traveller |

### What the actual game currently does (per baseline rules + audit)

This is the relevant honest-look question: which framing does the current game already commit to, mechanically?

- **Shared skill pool, alternating draft** — Pattern 1 from the research (Shared Vocabulary of Action). This is a Framing B feature already baked in. Both players draw from the same library, with a draft that makes the shared scarcity *visible*.
- **Perfect information, open skill loadouts** — Pattern 3 (Legible Opponent Puzzle). Already there. Both players can see what combos the other has access to.
- **No simultaneous-reveal moments** — Pattern 4 *not* present. Turns are strictly sequential. P1 then P2, no mutual commit.
- **No shared resource pool** — Pattern 2 *not* present. Each player has their own Runes; no DEFCON-style mutual penalty.
- **No "assessment phase" / structured pause** — explicit pauses for joint reckoning *not* present.

So the current chassis is **partially Framing-B-aligned** (shared vocabulary + legibility) but **not fully** (no simultaneous reveal, no shared scarcity, no assessment ritual). We're more Framing B than Framing A by accident of mechanics, but lightly.

### What the playtest data says (corrected for designer bias)

Honest answer: **almost nothing**, because the strongest piece of evidence we had — Jonathan's Q5 quote "thinking and **together**" — wasn't Jonathan's, it was Elias's. Per `feedback_designer_is_not_data.md`, that's been removed from the evidence pool.

What we *do* have, independent of Elias:

- **Pasco (P1):** chassis-first throughout. No evidence either way for shared-puzzle feel — the puzzle didn't land at all, so the question of *whose puzzle* didn't get tested.
- **Jonathan (P2):** one combo recognised late ("Shift + Focus + Blade + Medic, Turn 23"). His description of the *moment* was solo-puzzle-flavoured ("my combo, my favorite") — but this is one quote at the end of a 4-hour learning game, so it's weak signal in either direction.
- **Mario (P3):** played chassis-first, no combo experience. Same as Pasco — the question of "whose puzzle is this" never engaged because the puzzle didn't engage.

**Net:** we have zero independent evidence pointing toward Framing B specifically. We also have zero pointing toward Framing A. The shared-puzzle question is currently *untested*, not *unfavoured*.

### Where I'd land on this, with caveats

Reading the chassis honestly: the game *aspires* to Framing B and is partially set up for it. Three reasons:

1. **The shared draft pool is the most distinctive design choice in the game.** It's not "each player has their own deck." It's "we draft from one pile, alternating." That's a Framing B move at the architectural level. Throwing it away to lean A would erase one of the game's most identity-forming mechanics.
2. **Perfect information + open loadouts only matters if the opponent is supposed to be appreciated, not just predicted.** In Framing A, perfect info is purely strategic (no surprises = pure-skill outcome). In Framing B, perfect info is *aesthetic* (you watch the opponent build their combo, you see it coming, you appreciate the elegance when it lands). The current rules support both readings — but the second is more interesting and more distinctive.
3. **Framing B gives Phase B (theme/identity) a sharper brief.** "Two minds reading the same arcane structure" is a much more orientating brief than "wizard does spell combos." It rules out more themes and pulls more strongly toward visual language that emphasises mirroring, parallel discovery, mutual recognition.

**Caveat:** Framing B is *harder*. It requires the chassis to do work Framing A doesn't — combos must be legible to the opponent (not just the caster), the draft must produce roughly comparable puzzle-solving surfaces (asymmetry hurts more), and the "ah, of course" recognition has to be mechanically reachable in a single game. Mario's P3 didn't get there. Whether Framing B is *achievable* with the current chassis is partly what the on-ramp open questions in `high-concept-open-questions.md` are about.

### What this means in practice

If we tentatively adopt Framing B as the pitch (without yet committing it to design-principles.md or an ADR), it sharpens several existing open questions:

- **Q-B1 (starter loadouts):** Framing B argues for a *single* shared starter loadout for game 1, not two different ones — both players solve literally the same puzzle in their first game. Framing A would argue for mirrored-but-different loadouts (each player gets their own puzzle).
- **Q-B2 (combo hints on cards):** Framing B makes this more important — opponent legibility matters, so card text that telegraphs combo structure helps *both* players read the board.
- **Q-A1 (the framing decision itself):** this discussion is the input to that question. The answer that emerges here is what feeds into the eventual ADR or the one-paragraph design-principles.md edit.
- **Q-D1 (does the high concept land for new players?):** the framing changes what we're listening for in Nico's session. Framing A: "did Nico say he found a clever combo?" Framing B: "did Nico say something about *both players* exploring, racing, recognising each other's plays?" The second is a higher bar — but it's what the design is reaching for.
- **Phase B (Q-F1 — theme):** Framing B gives the theme brief. *Two minds, one combinatorial space* — divination, parallel oracle, dual-readers-of-the-same-text, mirrored mages. Framing A points toward more soloist themes (one wizard, one puzzle, opponent as antagonist).

### The "decide now or wait for data" question (deferred per user)

Holding this open as the user requested. The substantive question is whether Framing A vs B can be decided *from design intent alone*, or whether it should wait for Nico (and possibly more first-timer sessions) to tell us which framing the game actually delivers in practice. There's a real argument for each:

- **Decide now:** the framing is a designer's call about *what we're trying to build*, not an empirical measurement of *what the game currently delivers*. Waiting for data to "tell us" the framing risks the data telling us only what the chassis is currently doing — which may be Framing A by default, even though the design wants B.
- **Wait for data:** Framing B is a stronger claim and unconfirmed for any independent player. Adopting it now means treating an aspiration as a commitment, which then drives downstream design choices that may overshoot what the game can actually deliver.

We'll resolve this when we work through the open-questions doc — specifically alongside Q-A1 and Q-D1.

### Status

**Discussion logged.** No commitment yet. Working assumption: Framing B is where the game is reaching, and the chassis is partly set up for it. Whether to formalise that as a design-principles.md edit, an ADR, or to wait for Nico's data is open and will be decided when we work the open-questions list.

**Connected to:** Q-A1 (framing decision), Q-D1 (validation), Q-B1 / Q-B2 (on-ramp specifics that change with framing), Q-F1 (Phase B theme brief).

## Angle 4 — Chassis minimisation (theoretical)

**Format:** Theoretical analysis only. No rule changes proposed, no stack drafted, no ADR. The question is whether the current chassis is the *minimum-viable host* for the combo system, or whether it's bigger than it needs to be. Outputs of this angle feed into open questions and future design work — they don't trigger design work directly.

**Specificity guidepost:** *Less chassis = combos louder.* If a smaller chassis hosts the same combo experience, the simplification is pure win for the high concept. If the current chassis is already minimum-viable, this confirms it and we stop second-guessing.

**The current chassis (per `baseline-sections.typ`):** 10×10 grid · 1 King + 5 Champions + 6 Guards per player (12 pieces) · 15-skill catalogue · 2 Move Slots + 2 Skill Slots per turn (scaling) · Rune economy with starting 6 + scaling income · 2 HP track (Normal → Injured → Removed) · 3-cap Armor · Bodyguard interception · alternating skill draft.

### How to read this section

For each chassis dimension, three questions:

1. **What does this dimension contribute to the combo experience?** (the combo-serving share)
2. **What's the minimum-viable version that still hosts that contribution?** (the floor)
3. **Where is the current value relative to the floor?** (at-floor / above-floor / unclear)

Per audit, two dimensions already have flagged bloat candidates (Health & Armor volume, draft on-ramp). Those are recapped here in the chassis-minimisation lens; the rest are fresh.

---

### D1 — Board size (currently 10×10)

**Combo contribution:** Provides the spatial substrate combos play out across. Skills have Range 2 default; Skill Path is Queen-line; pieces block paths. The board needs to be big enough that:
- Setup-and-payoff turns can exist (caster needs space to position, target needs to be reachable).
- Skill Paths matter — too small a board and every piece is in line-of-sight of every other piece, the path-blocking subsystem becomes trivial.
- Movement matters separately from skills — too small and "move adjacent" makes Move skills redundant.

**Minimum-viable floor:** Hard to set without testing, but theoretically:
- Below ~6×6: Skill Path always reaches, blocking trivialises, movement collapses. Combat layer thins to "everyone hits everyone."
- 7×7 to 8×8: probably the floor for the current Range-2 default and 12 pieces — Skill Paths matter, but the board fills up fast.
- 10×10: comfortably above the floor; allows for *zones* (Mario's Armor wall on one side, Elias's combo line on the other in P3).

**Assessment:** **Above floor, but not obviously bloated.** The 10×10 size buys *zonal play* — different parts of the board can be doing different things simultaneously. Whether that's combo-serving (different combo theatres developing in parallel) or chassis-serving (more chess-style positioning) is the relevant question. Backpocket already has an "8×10 narrower variant" entry (per `high-concept-open-questions.md` Q-C3) — that's the right place to test this if it ever becomes priority.

**Connected to:** Q-C3 (chassis minimisation as a whole), OQ-1 (board size).

---

### D2 — Piece count and types (currently 1 King + 5 Champions + 6 Guards = 12 pieces per side)

**Combo contribution:**
- **Champions + King = 6 skill-carriers per side.** Each carries 2 skills → 12 skill instances per side, 24 on the table. This is the combo surface area.
- **Guards = 6 non-skill pieces per side.** They contribute to Bodyguard, blocking, and Standard Attack pressure. They do not carry skills, so they're chassis from the engine's perspective.

**Minimum-viable floor (engine side):**
- Need at least 2 skill-carriers per side for a multi-piece combo to even be possible (e.g., Focus Strike on one piece amplifying another's Strike). At 1 skill-carrier, all combos collapse to single-piece sequences, which the audit found are weaker as a combo experience (P3's organic Air Blast → Hook Pull was cross-piece).
- Probably want 3+ skill-carriers so the *cross-piece combo* lives — 2-piece combos are too easy to disrupt by removing one piece.
- 6 skill-carriers (current) is well above floor; gives draft variety, redundancy, and enough surface for genuinely emergent multi-piece combos.

**Minimum-viable floor (chassis side — Guards):**
- Bodyguard requires at least 1 Guard adjacent to the protected piece. Mechanically, you could have 1 or 2 Guards per side and Bodyguard would still function — but with limited density, Bodyguard rarely triggers (which was P1's complaint).
- 6 Guards (current) gives *enough density* that Bodyguard sometimes triggers. P3 had Bodyguard fire "way more often" than P1. The Guard count is calibrated to make Bodyguard *feel like a system*, not just an edge case.

**Assessment:** **Champions: above floor, probably appropriately so.** 6 skill-carriers is the combo system's actual content surface — shrinking this directly shrinks the engine. **Guards: above the strict mechanical floor (1 would work for Bodyguard to *exist*) but at-floor for Bodyguard to *feel like a system*.** Cutting Guards reduces a chassis subsystem the audit already flagged as the most chess-coded part of the game (Q-C2 on Bodyguard's vocabulary).

**Open question this raises (theoretical):** Is the Champion/Guard split the *right* shape, or is it a chess-inheritance? In a pure combo game, you might have *only* skill-carriers (6 pieces, all combat-relevant) and let interception be a skill rather than a piece-type. That's a more radical chassis cut than just shrinking numbers — it eliminates a whole piece category. Not proposing it, just naming that the Champion/Guard distinction is itself a chassis choice that could in principle be revisited.

**Connected to:** Q-C2 (Bodyguard framing), Q-C3 (chassis minimisation).

---

### D3 — Skill catalogue size (currently 15 skills across 4 categories)

**Combo contribution:** This is *the engine's content*. Catalogue size determines:
- **Combo space breadth:** how many distinct combo shapes are reachable. Narrower catalogue = fewer combos exist; wider = more, but harder to design well.
- **Draft variance:** how often a player's draft excludes combo-shaped skills. P3's Mario-2×Armorsmith outcome partly reflects narrow catalogue × bad draft.

**Minimum-viable floor:** Hard to set abstractly, but as a sanity check: for two players to each draft 12 skill instances with duplicates allowed and end up with combo-shaped loadouts *most of the time*, the catalogue probably needs:
- At least 3-4 *combo enablers* (skills whose value is mostly in amplifying other skills — currently Focus Strike, Blade Call, possibly Hook Pull as a setup).
- At least 6-8 *combo targets* (skills that benefit from being amplified — Strikes, mostly).
- Some chassis-flavoured skills to give drafts texture without being engine-irrelevant (Shields, Move).

The current catalogue is roughly: 5 Strikes (combo targets), 3 Shields (chassis-flavoured), 5 Moves (mixed — some combo-shaped like Air Blast, some chassis-shaped like Quick Dash), 2 Mystics (combo enablers).

**Assessment:** **Possibly at floor or slightly below for combo-shaped pairs.** Only 2 explicit combo enablers (Focus Strike, Blade Call), and 3 of 15 skills are pure chassis (Shields). If a player drafts away from the 2 enablers (Mario's draft did), the combo experience is harder to reach. Q-E1 in the open-questions doc is exactly this — *does the catalogue support enough combo discovery, or is it too narrow?*

**The chassis-minimisation lens adds a wrinkle:** if the catalogue grows to fix the combo-coverage problem (Q-E1), that's adding chassis volume. The trade-off is: more skills = more combo space, but also more learning load. The minimum-viable catalogue for the high concept is *as small as possible while still producing combo-shaped drafts reliably* — possibly that's larger than 15, possibly it's 15 with a different *shape* (more enablers, fewer chassis skills).

**Connected to:** Q-E1 (catalogue width), Q-E2 (card layout/legibility).

---

### D4 — Health & Armor system (currently 2-HP track + 3-cap Armor)

**Combo contribution:** Provides the *consequence layer* — a target for combos to act on, an RPS loop (Strike vs. Shield vs. Armor-Breaker) that combos slot into.

**Minimum-viable floor:**
- Combo system needs *some* damage track to act on, otherwise combos have no target. 1-HP (binary alive/removed) is technically minimum-viable but collapses Injured, removes the speed/range penalty layer, and turns every hit into a kill.
- Armor needs to exist for the Strike/Shield/Armor-Breaker RPS loop to exist — which is the *first non-trivial combo* most players encounter (Armor-Breaker setting up Strike).

**Assessment per audit:** **Strongest bloat candidate.** The audit already flagged Health & Armor as **`chassis-necessary` for the RPS loop, `chassis-bloat` for the bookkeeping volume.** The volume — 3-state HP track, 3-cap Armor stack, granting/removing mechanics, Injured range/speed penalties, edge cases on "self"/"adjacent" skills — exceeds what combos strictly require. Mario's P3 stacked Armor 20 times and never reached the engine.

**Where the floor probably lives:**
- 2 HP (Normal → Injured → Removed): probably at-floor. Going to 1 HP collapses too much.
- 3-cap Armor: above floor. Q-C1 already proposes testing 2-cap. The RPS structure survives at cap 2; the loop volume shrinks.
- Injured penalties (range −1, speed cap 1): probably above floor. Q-B5 in the open-questions doc speculates whether Injured's *teaching cost* exceeds its first-game gameplay payoff. Removing or hiding Injured is a first-game-only intervention idea.

**The chassis-minimisation read:** Health & Armor is doing *more bookkeeping* than the combo system needs as its consequence layer. Tightening the cap and/or simplifying first-game teaching are exactly the levers Q-C1 and Q-B5 propose.

**Connected to:** Q-C1 (Armor cap), Q-B5 (Injured teaching cost), OQ-11.

---

### D5 — Slot economy (currently 2 Move Slots + 2 Skill Slots, scaling)

**Combo contribution:** This is **directly combo-serving.** 2 Skill Slots per turn means players can chain 2 skills in a single turn — which is the minimum unit of "combo" by definition. The progression to 3/4/5 Skill Slots over rounds means *late-game turns are more combo-rich*.

**Minimum-viable floor:** 2 Skill Slots per turn is the minimum for in-turn combos to exist. 1 Skill Slot per turn collapses to single-skill activations and pushes all combos across turn boundaries (which is weaker; the opponent gets to react between every skill).

**Assessment:** **At floor, deliberately.** The 2-Skill-Slot baseline is the *combo unit*; the progression curve adds combo headroom over time. This dimension is not bloat — it's the engine's *clock*. Cutting it would directly cut the combo experience.

The 2 Move Slots side is more chassis-flavoured — it's the "chess move" part of the turn. Could in principle be reduced (1 Move Slot would still let pieces move and attack), but doing so would make positioning much slower and tighten the Movement Phase to the point where setup-and-attack turns become rarer.

**Connected to:** none directly — this is a confirmed-at-floor dimension.

---

### D6 — Rune economy (currently start 6, +2 → +3 → +4 → +5 by round)

**Combo contribution:** Runes are the *cost layer* — they make combos expensive and force prioritisation. Without scarcity, combos are free, and "spending tension" (G8 from `design-principles.md`) collapses.

**Minimum-viable floor:** A combo system needs *some* resource constraint to make combos meaningful. The exact numbers (start 6, +2/+3/+4/+5) are calibration choices, not chassis structure — they determine *when* combos become reachable, but the existence of a Rune economy is itself the chassis.

**Assessment per audit:** **`Dual` with heavy on-ramp teaching cost.** The economy is combo-serving for veterans (resource pressure makes combos meaningful) but a heavy first-contact load for newcomers (income table, scaling, no cap, starting amount).

**Chassis-minimisation read:** The Rune *system* is at floor — combos need a cost layer. The Rune *teaching surface* (income table, scaling rules) is above floor for first-game players. The audit's recommendation was to lower the *prominence* of the economy in rule explanation, not to cut the system. This dimension is not a candidate for trimming, just for re-framing first-contact.

**Connected to:** audit's per-system Resource Economy notes; no specific OQ flagged.

---

### D7 — Bodyguard / interception (currently adjacent Guard takes the hit)

**Combo contribution:** **Limited.** Bodyguard is a chassis-side defensive subsystem. The audit's verdict was that Bodyguard speaks war-chess vocabulary even when working as designed (Q-C2). It interacts with Standard Attack only, not skills — so combos bypass it entirely.

**Minimum-viable floor:** The combo system does not require Bodyguard to exist. Standard Attack itself is mostly chassis (audit Q-B4 — its *framing* in rule text reinforces chassis-first thinking).

**Assessment:** **Above floor for the engine — Bodyguard is purely chassis.** That doesn't mean it should be cut. Bodyguard provides *strategic decisions* (where to position Guards, when to break a Guard wall) and contributes to the Standard Attack ↔ Skill ladder (skills bypass Bodyguard, which is part of why skills feel powerful). But from a pure combo-minimum-viable-chassis lens, Bodyguard is an above-floor chassis subsystem.

**The chassis-minimisation read with caveat:** Cutting Bodyguard would cut a real subsystem and cost the game more than it gains for the combo experience. The audit's Q-C2 doesn't ask to *remove* Bodyguard — it asks whether its *vocabulary* could be reframed (non-chess name, perhaps reframed around skill interactions rather than just attacks). That's a Phase B (theme/identity) question more than a chassis-cutting question.

**Connected to:** Q-C2 (Bodyguard framing), Q-B4 (Standard Attack framing).

---

### D8 — Drafting mechanic (currently alternating-pick from shared pool)

**Combo contribution:** **Highly combo-serving in design intent** (per audit) — drafting is where combos are *built*. Players choose which skills go on which Champions, which determines what combos are available.

**Minimum-viable floor:** Combos need *some* loadout-construction step. The minimum is "skills are pre-assigned" (no draft) — which would lose all the strategic on-ramp. So drafting in some form is at-floor for the high concept.

**Assessment per audit:** **`Dual` — the system itself is combo-serving, but its on-ramp delivery is the problem.** New players draft *before* they understand combos. That's not a chassis-bloat problem — it's a sequencing problem with the on-ramp (Q-B1, Q-B2, Q-B3 all address this).

**Chassis-minimisation read:** The draft *system* is at floor. The draft *as first-game experience* is above floor for newcomers (too much decision before they have the intuition to make it). The minimum-viable draft for game 1 might be a starter loadout (Q-B1) or no-draft-yet (Q-B3) — but that's an on-ramp question, not a chassis-cutting question.

**Connected to:** Q-B1 / Q-B2 / Q-B3.

### Headline findings

1. **Most chassis dimensions are at-floor or close to it.** D5 (Skill Slots), D6 (Rune system), D8 (draft system) are at-floor as systems. D2 Champions count is deliberately above-floor because Champions *are* the combo surface — shrinking them shrinks the engine. D3 (catalogue) may even be *below* floor for combo coverage.

2. **The two clear chassis-bloat candidates are both volume-side, not system-side.** D4 (Health & Armor bookkeeping) and D8's first-game teaching surface are above floor. Cutting them is *trimming* not *removing* — Armor cap 3→2, Injured-hidden first game, simpler economy explanation. These are exactly the levers Q-C1, Q-B5, Q-B1/2/3 already propose.

3. **The "less chassis = combos louder" principle resolves to volume management, not system cuts.** No system in the audit (or this analysis) is pure bloat that should be removed. Every dimension pulls some weight. The opportunity space is: tighter caps, simpler first-contact framing, on-ramp interventions that reduce teaching load — not cutting whole systems.

4. **D2 Guards is the most defensible "could in principle be cut" candidate from a pure-combo lens** — because Guards don't carry skills. But the audit already noted Bodyguard provides strategic-decision content even though it's chassis-coded. Cutting Guards would lose Bodyguard, attack-pressure density, and the King-target chess-feel that the chassis (correctly) provides. Theoretical possibility, not a recommendation.

5. **D1 Board size is the biggest unknown.** 10×10 is above floor in some abstract sense, but whether 8×8 or 8×10 would deliver the same combo experience is genuinely untested. This is the main thing Q-C3 / Angle-4-as-stack-work would resolve. Until then, theoretical.

6. **Chassis minimisation and the on-ramp problem are the same problem viewed from different angles.** What the audit called "chassis-bloat" overlaps almost entirely with what Q-B* called "on-ramp interventions." The bloat *is* the teaching surface for first-game players. Trimming chassis volume = trimming on-ramp cost. The two work-streams are siblings.

### What this means for the project

- **No new design work proposed.** All the levers this analysis surfaces are already in the open-questions doc (Q-C1, Q-C2, Q-C3, Q-B1-5, Q-E1, Q-E2). The angle's contribution is a *frame*, not a new candidate stack.
- **The chassis is mostly minimum-viable already.** That's a useful reassurance — we should stop second-guessing whether the chassis is fundamentally too big. The available wins are at the volume/teaching margin, not at the system-architecture level.
- **"Specificity = less chassis louder combos" is best read as 'tighten the volume of chassis-necessary systems,' not 'cut chassis systems.'** That's a meaningful clarification for future design discussions: the rule isn't *delete*, it's *quiet down*.
- **The Phase B (theme/identity) work has more leverage than further chassis-cutting.** Reframing chess-coded vocabulary (Bodyguard, Standard Attack), making the engine *audible* through visual/naming choices, etc., are bigger wins than further mechanical reduction at this point.
